The **pay scale** structures a company's compensation policy by linking each category, level or coefficient to a minimum salary amount. Used in large organizations and SMEs alike, it serves as a reference for setting hiring offers, arbitrating raises, and ensuring pay consistency between employees in comparable roles. Its design rests both on legal and collective bargaining obligations and on choices specific to each company's compensation policy.

## What Is a Pay Scale?

A pay scale is a **compensation management tool** that classifies a company's roles by category, level or coefficient, and assigns each cell a minimum amount, sometimes a maximum one too. It can be negotiated at industry level, through the applicable collective bargaining agreement, or built internally by the company to go beyond the agreement's minimums.

Its role goes beyond simply setting a hiring amount. A well-built pay scale acts as a shared reference between management, line managers and the HR function to make compensation decisions more objective, whether for a hire, a promotion or an annual review. It limits unjustified pay gaps between employees in comparable roles and makes mandatory annual pay negotiations easier to conduct.

### Pay Scale, Statutory Minimum Wage, and Collective Agreement Minimum: What to Distinguish

Three concepts are often confused. The **statutory minimum wage** is the legal minimum salary, applicable to all employees regardless of sector. The collective agreement minimum corresponds to the minimum amount set by the sector agreement for each category or coefficient: it can never be lower than the statutory minimum, but it is frequently higher for the most qualified categories. The company's own pay scale, finally, may strictly mirror the agreement's minimums or exceed them, depending on the compensation policy chosen.

An employer can never apply a salary below the collective agreement minimum applicable to the employee's category, even when that amount exceeds the statutory minimum wage. Checking consistency between the internal scale, the agreement minimum and the statutory minimum is therefore a first-level control before any hire.

## Is a Pay Scale Mandatory for a Company?

No legal provision requires a company to formalize its own pay scale. However, applying the **collective agreement minimums** set by the sector agreement is mandatory as soon as the company falls within its scope. In practice, almost every employer is therefore forced, at least indirectly, to respect a minimum pay scale, even without having formalized one internally.

Formalizing an internal scale remains strongly recommended past a certain headcount: it secures equal treatment between employees, makes labor inspection checks easier, and reduces the risk of pay discrimination disputes. Companies subject to the new [pay transparency](/en/blog/pay-transparency/) requirements have every reason to formalize a readable scale, one able to justify each level of compensation applied.

## How Do You Read a Pay Scale? Levels, Coefficients and Steps

Reading a pay scale varies depending on the reference system used by the applicable collective agreement, but it generally rests on a combination of two axes:

- a **level** or **category**, which reflects the nature of the role and its degree of responsibility (employee, supervisor, manager, for example);
- a **coefficient** or **step**, which refines the employee's position within their category, often based on seniority, qualification, or experience gained.

The amount attached to each coefficient corresponds to the minimum gross monthly or hourly salary the employer must respect for that level. An employee can therefore identify their own position by cross-referencing their job title, their contractual classification, and the correspondence table published by their industry.

Simplified example of a pay scale structure, to be adapted to the collective agreement actually applicable:

| Category | Coefficient | Minimum gross monthly salary (35h base) |
|---|---|---|
| Employee, level 1 | 140 | Statutory or agreement minimum |
| Employee, level 2 | 150 to 160 | + 2 to 5% above level 1 |
| Supervisor | 200 to 230 | + 10 to 20% depending on coefficient |
| Manager | 300 and above | Flat-rate or dedicated manager scale |

This table is illustrative only: every collective agreement sets its own category labels, coefficients and associated amounts, regularly revalued through salary amendments.

## Pay Scale by Industry and Collective Agreement

Pay scales vary widely from one industry to another, depending on the structure of roles, the qualification level required, and the negotiations specific to each sector. The same job title can therefore correspond to very different amounts depending on whether the company falls under manufacturing, retail, hospitality, or the healthcare and social sector.

### Pay Scale and Collective Agreement: Where to Find the Right Rates

Every collective agreement publishes its own scale, identifiable by its **IDCC** code, and updates it periodically through salary revaluation amendments. Applicable scales are available on Légifrance, on the relevant industry federation's website, or directly from HR departments for companies that have internalized this monitoring. For regulated occupations and the hospital, local government and state civil services, the statutory grade logic complements this system with its own rules.

### Civil Service Statutory Grade: A Special Case

In the civil service, pay does not follow a collective bargaining agreement but a **statutory grade**, common to each status category (A, B, C). Each step corresponds to a weighted index, converted into gross monthly salary via a point value set nationally. Step advancement mainly depends on seniority, with minimum and maximum durations defined by corps or job category. This mechanism differs significantly from the private sector's bargaining logic, but serves the same purpose: guaranteeing pay consistency among staff in comparable roles.

## What Are the Benefits of a Pay Scale?

Beyond its sometimes mandatory nature, a well-built pay scale brings concrete benefits, both for the employer and for employees.

### For the Company

A pay scale structures compensation policy and limits case-by-case, arbitrary decisions. It makes payroll cost management easier, giving visibility into the cost of each hire or promotion before negotiations even open. It also protects the company against legal risk: in the event of an inspection or dispute, a documented scale allows each level of compensation applied to be objectively justified.

### For Employees

For employees, the pay scale brings clarity on career prospects: it makes it possible to identify the pay thresholds tied to taking on responsibility, additional seniority, or skill development. It also serves as a safeguard against unjustified pay gaps between colleagues in comparable roles, an issue reinforced by growing pay transparency requirements.

## Setting Up or Updating a Pay Scale with HRIS Software

Building a pay scale by hand, from scattered spreadsheets, quickly becomes a source of errors once a company grows past a few dozen employees: a missed agreement revaluation, an inconsistency between two sites, or a scale that was not updated after a salary amendment. [Payroll software](/en/blog/payroll-software-5-criteria/) or a dedicated HRIS compensation module makes it possible to centralize the scale, automatically flag any gap with the applicable agreement minimum, and simulate the budget impact of a revaluation before rolling it out.

The same tool also makes it easier to connect with other HR processes: classifying an employee from the [prior hiring declaration](/en/blog/prior-hiring-declaration-france/) onward, tracking raises during annual reviews, or preparing a salary range before publishing a job posting. Centralizing compensation data in a single system reduces the risk of error and speeds up producing the records requested by labor inspectors or employee representatives.
