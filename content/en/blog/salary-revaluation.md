A **salary revaluation** is an upward adjustment to the pay of an employee, a role, or a category of staff. It can stem from a legal obligation, such as a minimum wage increase, from a collective bargaining clause, or from a company decision that recognizes performance, added responsibility, or corrects a gap with the market. For HR teams, it raises questions of legal framework, method, and fairness between employees.

## What Is a Salary Revaluation?

Salary revaluation refers to any increase in an employee's base pay decided after hiring. It differs from setting the initial salary at the time the contract is signed, and it covers very different situations: a mechanical adjustment tied to a minimum wage increase, an individual raise decided during an annual review, or a collective revision negotiated with employee representatives.

The term covers both a **unilateral** employer decision and an agreement reached through **negotiation**. In both cases, the revaluation permanently changes the base salary, unlike a one-off bonus that only applies to a given period. This distinction matters for HR tracking: a revaluation becomes part of the reference salary and affects every calculation that depends on it, from severance pay to social contributions to the baseline for a future raise.

In HR practice, three families of revaluation are generally distinguished: one imposed by law or a collective bargaining agreement, one negotiated collectively during mandatory annual negotiations, and one decided individually for a specific employee.

## Is Salary Revaluation Mandatory?

### Cases Where the Law Requires a Revaluation

An employer must revalue a salary in specific situations. The first concerns the **minimum wage**: each increase in the minimum wage requires adjusting any contract that would otherwise mechanically fall below the new threshold. The second stems from **collective bargaining agreements**, which set minimum pay scales by grade or job category; a base salary below this contractual minimum must be brought up to standard, regardless of the employer's intent.

**Mandatory annual negotiations** create an obligation to negotiate in companies with union representatives, but not an obligation of result: the employer must open negotiations on pay, without being required to grant an increase if the discussions do not lead to an agreement.

### Cases Where the Revaluation Remains at the Employer's Discretion

Outside these legal and contractual obligations, employers retain broad discretion. No law requires individually raising a high-performing employee's pay, nor following an internal scale beyond collective bargaining minimums. This discretion explains why revaluation policy varies widely from one company to another, depending on size, sector, and financial situation.

Certain contractual commitments or company agreements can nonetheless create an obligation specific to the employer, for example an indexation clause tied to a given index or a commitment made during a previous negotiation.

## When and Why Revalue a Salary?

### During the Annual Review

The **annual review** remains the most common moment to discuss an individual revaluation. The manager reviews the objectives achieved and any changes in scope, which provides a concrete basis to justify or decline a raise. Recording this discussion in a dedicated tool helps keep track of commitments made and follow up on them from one review cycle to the next.

### After a Promotion or a Role Change

A role change, added responsibility, or an internal move generally comes with a revaluation, to align pay with the new level of complexity and responsibility. Without it, the gap between the role's actual workload and the pay received becomes a frequent source of departures.

### To Correct a Gap With the Market

A salary that falls behind the market, due to a lack of regular review, exposes the company to turnover risk on its most sought-after roles. A periodic salary benchmark helps identify these gaps before they turn into a resignation, rather than discovering the pay gap only when the employee announces they are leaving.

## What Criteria Help Assess a Salary Revaluation?

Several criteria, often combined, help make a revaluation decision more objective rather than handling it case by case:

| Criterion | What It Measures | Limitation to Keep in Mind |
|---|---|---|
| Individual performance | Achievement of objectives set during the review | Requires measurable, documented objectives |
| Seniority | Retention and skill growth in the role | Does not always reflect actual contribution |
| Market positioning | Gap between current pay and sector practice | Depends on the quality of benchmark data |
| Expanded scope | New responsibilities assigned to the employee | Must be formalized to avoid disputes |
| Budget constraint | Overall envelope available for raises | Can limit an otherwise justified revaluation |

None of these criteria is sufficient on its own: a company that based decisions on seniority alone, for example, would risk demotivating its highest-performing recent hires.

## How Do You Run a Salary Revaluation Campaign?

### The Key Steps of the Process

A revaluation campaign generally follows a multi-step method. The company first defines an **overall budget envelope**, often expressed as a percentage of total payroll. Managers then propose individual raises within that envelope, based on the chosen criteria. The HR department arbitrates the requests, checks consistency across departments, and approves the campaign before communicating it to the employees concerned.

### Tracking the Budget Envelope

Tracking the envelope is the most sensitive part of the process: without consolidated visibility, a company can grant more raises early in the campaign than its budget allows, at the expense of teams processed later in the schedule. A centralized dashboard, updated as decisions are made, prevents this imbalance between departments and helps communicate a consistent decision to all managers involved.

## How Do You Ask for a Salary Revaluation as an Employee?

A revaluation request is more effective when it rests on facts rather than a simple feeling. A quantified summary of results achieved, a list of responsibilities actually carried out beyond the initial job description, or a sourced comparison with market practice gives the manager a basis to review.

Timing also matters: the annual review remains the most natural setting, but a significant event (a new responsibility taken on during the year, an outstanding result) can justify requesting a dedicated conversation without waiting for that date. If the request is declined, asking for the specific criteria behind the decision helps build a stronger case for the next campaign, rather than repeating the same request without any new element.

## What Role Does HRIS Software Play in Managing Salary Revaluation?

Managing a revaluation campaign on scattered spreadsheets multiplies the risk of error once headcount passes a few dozen employees: an envelope overrun that goes unnoticed in time, inconsistency between two managers on comparable roles, or a lack of traceability for the criteria used in case of a dispute. A dedicated module, built into [payroll software](/en/blog/payroll-software-5-criteria/) or a broader HRIS, centralizes requests, simulates the budget impact before approval, and keeps a usable history from one campaign to the next.

This centralization also makes it easier to connect with other HR processes: decisions made during the annual review feed directly into the revaluation record, the [pay scale](/en/blog/pay-scale/) serves as a reference to check the consistency of each proposal, and the [compensation plan](/en/blog/compensation-plan/) sets the overall framework each individual decision fits into. It also reduces the risk tied to growing [pay transparency](/en/blog/pay-transparency/) requirements, which require being able to objectively justify every pay gap observed between comparable roles.
